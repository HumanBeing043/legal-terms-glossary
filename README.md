# Legal Terms Glossary

A plain-English reference for common legal terms that show up often in court reporting,
compliance documentation, and contract review — written for people who aren't lawyers but
need to understand what these words actually mean in practice.

Contributions and corrections welcome. This is a living reference, not legal advice.

## Litigant

A litigant is a party actively involved in a lawsuit — either the person or entity bringing
the claim (the plaintiff) or the one defending against it (the defendant). The term is purely
descriptive of procedural status and carries no implication about who is in the right.
Corporations, government agencies, and individuals can all be litigants in exactly the same
technical sense.

**Further reading:** [Litigant status in civil proceedings](https://wd8rrg.litigant.world/tckdyczxpyd.html)

## Indictment

An indictment is a formal written accusation of a serious crime, typically issued after a
grand jury reviews evidence presented by a prosecutor and finds it sufficient to proceed to
trial. It is not a finding of guilt — it marks the formal start of a criminal prosecution.
In the U.S. federal system, an indictment is constitutionally required for most serious
federal offenses; many states allow prosecution by information instead.

## Chancery

Chancery refers to a court system rooted in English equity law — historically separate from
common law courts, and empowered to grant remedies common law couldn't, like injunctions and
specific performance. England merged its chancery and common law courts procedurally in the
1870s, but the tradition survives in a few U.S. states; Delaware's Court of Chancery in
particular remains highly influential in American corporate law.

## Edict

An edict is an official order or proclamation issued by an authority with the power to
enforce it — historically a ruler or government body, though the term is also used more
loosely today for any binding directive issued from a position of authority. Unlike
legislation, which typically goes through a deliberative process, an edict is issued
directly and takes effect by the authority of the issuer.

## Injunction

An injunction is a court order requiring a party to do, or refrain from doing, a specific
act. Unlike monetary damages, an injunction is an equitable remedy — a court will typically
only grant one when money alone wouldn't adequately address the harm. Injunctions can be
temporary (to preserve the status quo while a case proceeds) or permanent (issued as part of
a final judgment).

**Further reading:** [When courts grant injunctive relief](https://8ep0ny.injunction.world/rrkrptyhcxc.html)
